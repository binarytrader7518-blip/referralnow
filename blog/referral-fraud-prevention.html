Referral Fraud: Practical Safeguards for Small Businesses

Self-referrals, recycled codes and refunded orders. The common abuse patterns and the simple rules that stop them.

Published 10 November 2026

Any program that pays people can attract people who game it. The good news is that most referral fraud follows a few predictable patterns, and simple rules stop the majority of it.

The common patterns

  • Self-referral: a customer refers themselves using a second email or phone number to claim both rewards.
  • Code sharing: a discount code ends up on coupon sites, so “referrals” come from strangers.
  • Buy-and-refund: a friend buys to trigger the reward, then asks for a refund.
  • Existing customers: people who already shop with you claim a new-customer offer.

Rules that stop most of it

  1. Reward confirmed purchases only, never clicks or sign-ups.
  2. Hold rewards through your refund window, and cancel rewards for refunded sales.
  3. Use personal links and codes, so every claim traces back to one advocate.
  4. Check for matching details, such as the same surname, device or address as the advocate.
  5. Require a person to approve cash rewards, which are the most attractive to abuse.
  6. Set a budget ceiling so an unexpected spike can't run up a large bill.

Keep it friendly

Most of your advocates are honest. Write your terms in plain English, explain why a claim was declined, and give genuine customers an easy way to ask questions.
